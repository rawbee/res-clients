01

The mental model: what kind of system this is

Critical

Foundation for everything else. Read once, then never be surprised again.

Salesforce is not a database you build on. It is a multitenant application whose configuration is data, running your code inside hard quotas on shared infrastructure. Almost every counterintuitive thing about the platform follows from that one sentence.

Your instance is an org — a tenant. Everything you “build” is metadata: objects, fields, page layouts, validation rules, flows, permission sets, report types. Metadata is versionable XML you can pull, diff, and deploy like source. Records are the actual rows your users create. The two move on completely different rails: metadata deploys from sandbox to production, data does not travel with it.

The decision ladder every Salesforce professional uses

When someone asks “can you make X happen,” the platform has a settled order of preference. Naming this ladder out loud is one of the fastest credibility signals available to us, and it is already our stated philosophy in the proposal: native integrations before middleware, middleware before custom code.

1Configuration. A field, a picklist, a validation rule, a page layout. No code, no deploy risk, any admin can maintain it. Always ask whether the answer lives here first.
2Declarative automation — Flow. Record-triggered and scheduled flows cover the overwhelming majority of routing, field updates, and cross-object logic. Version-controlled, debuggable, and the client’s new CRM admin can read it.
3Native connectors. The HubSpot↔Salesforce package, native ad-platform syncs, Calendly/HubSpot Meetings integrations. Supported by the vendor, monitored by the vendor, upgraded by the vendor.
4Middleware. Zapier, Make, Workato. Fast to build, but every automation is another thing that fails silently and another line item on somebody’s card. Use where no native path exists.
5Apex / custom code. Last resort. Requires 75% test coverage to deploy to production, requires a developer forever, and is the single most common way a small nonprofit ends up locked to a vendor. If we write code, we document it and hand it over.
Why this ladder wins us points

Decision D5 in the README: we position as the architecture layer above their incoming CRM Administrator, not as a competing pair of hands. The ladder is that positioning made concrete — every rung we stay above is a thing their admin can own after we leave. Reach for Apex and you have quietly built dependency.

Governor limits, in one paragraph

Because thousands of tenants share the same application servers, Salesforce kills any transaction that consumes too much. The numbers you should be able to recite are in the cheat sheet, but the principle is what matters in conversation: everything must be written in bulk. Code or flows that work on one record and blow up on two hundred are the classic novice failure, and “their data load fails at scale” is a symptom we should expect to find in an org that grew by accretion.

03

Lead conversion: the one-way door

Critical

Serves tech-brief problem #2 marketing and the CRM disagree about who a lead is — and it is the single highest-value thing to understand cold.

A Lead is a deliberately anomalous object: a person and a company crammed into one flat record, with no relationships to anything. That is by design — before qualification you do not yet know whether this person belongs to a company you already track. Conversion is the moment you decide.

Converting a Lead does three things at once, and it cannot be undone through the UI.

This is the attribution killer

The click ID captured on the form (module 08) lives on the Lead. If gclid__c has no conversion mapping to a Contact field and an Opportunity field, the thread from ad click to landed project is cut at exactly step 4 of the loop. Organizations discover this months later, when someone asks why no closed project has a campaign attached. When we audit an org, Setup → Object Manager → Lead → Fields → Map Lead Fields is one of the first screens to open.

What else conversion does

  • Account matching is not automatic. Out of the box, the converting user picks an existing Account or creates a new one from the Lead’s Company text. “Acme Biotech” and “Acme Biotech, LLC” become two Accounts unless someone intervenes — this is precisely the mess in the tech brief’s before/after demo.
  • Campaign membership carries over. The Lead’s CampaignMember rows re-point to the new Contact, which is what keeps campaign attribution alive across the conversion.
  • Activities carry over to the Contact, so the history is preserved.
  • The Lead becomes read-only with IsConverted = true. It still exists, still counts against storage, and — importantly — is excluded from most Lead reports by default, which is a common cause of two dashboards disagreeing.
  • Conversion can fire automation. Record-triggered flows on Contact/Account/Opportunity creation all fire, as do validation rules — a required field on Opportunity that the Lead has no value for will make conversion fail outright.
The strategic question underneath

Many organizations debate whether to use Leads at all versus creating Contacts directly. For an EDO, Leads earn their place: a site selector who fills out a report download is not yet attached to a real project. But the rules for when a Lead converts must be written down and agreed with BD — otherwise “qualified” means something different in every conversation, which is tech-brief problem #2 in one sentence.

04

Fields, picklists, record types, validation rules

Critical

Serves tech-brief problem #1 the data foundation is almost — but not — done. This module is the Data Health Review.

“Three fields hold the same thing. Puerto Rico is spelled four ways.” Every word of that complaint is a fact about field metadata. Here is the vocabulary for fixing it.

ConceptWhat it isWhat it means for the cleanup
Label vs. API name Every field has a human label and an immutable API name. Custom fields end in __c. Two fields can carry the same label and different API names — that is how “three fields hold the same thing” survives a visual audit. Audit by API name, always.
Picklist A constrained value set. Can be restricted (nothing outside the list) or unrestricted (the API can write anything). An unrestricted picklist is free text wearing a costume — an integration can inject “puerto rico, usa” right past it. Restricting picklists is usually step one.
Global value set One value list shared by picklists across many objects. The mechanism that makes Country mean the same thing on Lead, Contact, and Account. Without it you maintain four lists and they drift.
Multi-select picklist Semicolon-delimited multiple values in one field. Notoriously painful to report and filter on. If we find sector stored this way, flag it — it is usually why a report “quietly misses records.”
Record type A variant of an object: different page layout, different subset of picklist values, different business process. A record type can make a picklist value invisible on one record type and valid on another. This is a top-three cause of integration sync failures that “work fine when I do it by hand.”
Validation rule A formula evaluated on save. If it returns TRUE, the save is blocked with an error. Validation rules apply to API writes too. A well-meaning “Country is required” rule will reject every HubSpot sync that has no country. See module 06.
Formula field Calculated at read time, never stored, cannot be written to. Cheap normalization: derive a clean “Region” from a messy Country without touching source data. But you cannot sync into one, and integrations that try will error.
Roll-up summary COUNT / SUM / MIN / MAX of child records onto a parent. Master-detail relationships only. How “total capital in pipeline per Account” gets computed without code. Lookup relationships cannot do this natively — that gap is why orgs end up with Apex.
External ID A custom field flagged as an external unique key, indexed by the platform. The single most important field type for integration work. It enables upsert: “create if absent, update if present, match on this key.” Without external IDs, every re-run of a sync creates duplicates.
Field history tracking Up to 20 fields per object logged with old/new value and timestamp. Turn it on for the fields that drive routing and reporting before we start changing things. It is how you answer “who changed this and when” six weeks later.

How you retire a field without breaking anything

You do not delete it. You (1) find every dependency — Setup has a Where is this used? view on each field, plus reports, flows, and page layouts; (2) migrate the values into the surviving field; (3) remove it from all layouts and revoke field-level security so nobody can see it; (4) rename the label to ZZ_DEPRECATED_…; (5) delete only after a full reporting cycle has passed. Deleted fields sit in a recycle bin for 15 days and then the data is unrecoverable.

05

Duplicates, merging, and getting data in safely

Critical

Serves tech-brief problem #1. The differentiator we claim is scheduled automated cleanup, not one-time manual fixes — this module is the evidence.

The native mechanism, in two parts

Matching rules — “what counts as the same?”

A matching rule defines the comparison: exact match on Email, fuzzy match on First Name, exact on Company. Salesforce ships standard rules for Lead, Contact, and Account; you can write custom ones.

Key limit

Matching runs across Leads↔Contacts too, which matters enormously here — the same site selector often exists as an old Lead and a current Contact.

Duplicate rules — “what do we do about it?”

A duplicate rule binds a matching rule to an action: Allow (log it), Alert (warn the user, let them proceed), or Block. It can be configured to bypass the rule for specific profiles — and, crucially, you choose whether it applies to API-created records.

Key limit

Native rules prevent new duplicates. They do not clean up the existing pile, and they cannot merge on a schedule.

The trap that hits integrations

Set a duplicate rule to Block and the HubSpot connector’s record creation starts failing — the errors land in a sync queue nobody is watching, and marketing concludes “the sync is broken.” The right answer is usually Block for manual entry, Allow-with-report for the integration user, plus a scheduled dedupe pass. Being able to say that sentence is worth more than any tool name.

What actually happens on merge

  • You pick a master; the master’s field values win by default, though you can choose field-by-field in the UI.
  • Child records — activities, campaign memberships, opportunities, attachments — re-parent to the master. Nothing is lost there.
  • Field values on the losing records that you did not select are discarded permanently. If the loser held the only gclid__c, attribution dies in the merge.
  • The losing record IDs are deleted. Anything integrating on those IDs — a HubSpot record mapping, a Zap, a spreadsheet — now points at nothing.
  • The UI merges three records at a time. Real cleanup at volume needs tooling.

Tooling — and the honest reason to name one

ToolWhat it is forReality check
Data Import WizardIn-app, up to 50,000 records, respects duplicate rulesFine for a small list upload by a trained user. This is what the SOP for “list uploads” should describe.
Data LoaderDesktop/CLI, millions of records via Bulk API, supports upsert on External IDThe engineer’s tool. Always export first, always dry-run in a sandbox.

Naming a specific tool in the proposal is deliberate: it converts “we will improve data quality” into a costed, verifiable commitment. It also invites the question “why that one,” so have the answer — for a two-sided HubSpot/Salesforce problem on a nonprofit budget, a tool that operates on both systems beats a Salesforce-only one.

The before/after report is the deliverable

The tech brief’s interactive demo — five messy records, five clean ones — is the shape of the evidence we hand over: record counts by field completeness, duplicate clusters found and merged, picklist values collapsed, and the filter that used to return 1 of 3 Puerto Rico records now returning 3 of 3. Run those counts before touching anything. You cannot reconstruct a baseline after you have fixed it.

06

The HubSpot ↔ Salesforce seam

Critical

Serves tech-brief problem #2. Per the tech brief, the RFP’s whole problem list “reads like a textbook description of a drifted HubSpot↔Salesforce integration.” This is the battleground.

The connector is a managed package installed in Salesforce plus a settings surface in HubSpot. It authenticates as one Salesforce user and moves records both ways on a near-continuous cycle. Everything that goes wrong with it goes wrong in one of four places.

Gate 1 — the inclusion list

HubSpot can gate which contacts are permitted to sync to Salesforce using an active list. Without one, every conference badge scan, every purchased list, and every newsletter subscriber lands in the CRM. With one, the CRM contains only records the business has a reason to hold. Asking “do you have an inclusion list, and what is its definition?” is a question no generalist vendor asks, and the answer tells you immediately how disciplined the org is.

Gate 2 — field mappings and the ownership rule

Each mapped field carries a sync behavior: two-way, two-way with Salesforce preferred on conflict, or one-way from Salesforce into HubSpot. (Verify the current option labels in the connector settings before quoting them — they have changed over the years.) The engineering rule underneath is invariant and worth stating in the meeting:

The rule

One field, one owner system, written down. A field that both systems can write is a field that will eventually disagree with itself, and no amount of monitoring will tell you which value was right. Two-way sync is a legitimate choice for a handful of fields; it is a catastrophe as a default.

The specific mappings that cause trouble at an EDO:

  • Lifecycle Stage (HubSpot) vs. Lead Status (Salesforce) vs. Opportunity Stage. Three different vocabularies for “how far along is this,” maintained by two different teams. They must be mapped explicitly, value by value, with a written definition of each — and HubSpot lifecycle stages do not move backwards by default, which surprises people.
  • Lead Source. Almost always overwritten by the wrong side. First-touch attribution dies here.
  • Owner. HubSpot owner and Salesforce OwnerId are different user records; mapping them requires the same person to exist in both systems, correctly linked.
  • Picklist values. If HubSpot sends “Life Sciences” and Salesforce’s restricted picklist only has “Biotech,” the write fails. Every time.

Gate 3 — the integration user

The connector acts as one Salesforce user. That user’s profile, permission sets, field-level security, role, and sharing access define the ceiling on what the sync can do. Three things follow:

  • It should be a dedicated user, not a person’s account. Otherwise the audit trail says an employee edited 40,000 records, and when they leave and their user is deactivated the sync dies.
  • If a field is hidden from that user by field-level security, the sync silently cannot write it — no error, just a permanently empty field.
  • If org-wide defaults are Private and the integration user has no role high enough to see a record, the connector cannot update it.

Gate 4 — triage the error queue

And the connector cannot fix what it cannot see: records created directly in Salesforce before the connector was installed may never have been enrolled, which is why the marketing tool and the CRM can hold wildly different totals with no errors at all.

08

The attribution loop, in Salesforce terms

Critical

Serves tech-brief problem #3 ad spend can’t see which leads became real projects — described in the brief as “the most valuable single fix in this RFP.”

The tech brief walks the loop conceptually. This is the same journey with the object, field, and setting named at every step — which is what a technical stakeholder will actually press us on.

Salesforce’s own campaign machinery

Primary Campaign Source

A single lookup field on Opportunity. It drives the standard campaign ROI report and is what most orgs mean when they say “attribution.” It is single-touch — the last campaign to touch the record before conversion, or whatever a human last set.

Campaign Influence

An optional feature that credits multiple campaigns per Opportunity under a configurable model. Better matches an EDO reality where a project touched an ad, a webinar, and a trade mission over eighteen months. Must be explicitly enabled, and adds real complexity — recommend it deliberately, not reflexively.

Campaign hierarchy

Campaigns nest up to five levels, with roll-up fields for the whole branch. The natural structure for an EDO: Fiscal year → channel → market/sector → individual push. Getting this hierarchy right at the start is cheaper than restructuring it later.

CampaignMember status

Per-campaign statuses — Sent, Responded, Registered, Attended — with one flagged as “Responded.” Customizing these per campaign type is how event attendance becomes measurable rather than anecdotal.

The timely detail that signals we are current

Per our tech brief research: Google is retiring the legacy click-ID upload path (UploadClickConversions) on June 15, 2026 — mid-engagement — in favour of Enhanced Conversions for Leads and the Data Manager API, and Meta has folded its old Offline Events tool into the Conversions API. Mentioning that we plan for these migrations is the difference between reading as current and reading as boilerplate. Note also that Enhanced Conversions for Leads matches on hashed customer data (email, phone) rather than requiring the click ID — so capturing email cleanly is itself an attribution requirement.

Where this breaks in practice, in order of likelihood
  1. Hidden click-ID fields were never added to the form, so there is nothing to capture.
  2. The fields exist on Lead but have no lead conversion mapping (module 03), so the value dies at qualification.
  3. The field mapping in the connector is one-way in the wrong direction, so the CRM never receives it.
  4. Nobody defined which CRM stage counts as the conversion event worth sending back — so the feedback either never starts or sends noise.
  5. Consent and privacy handling for sending hashed PII to ad platforms was never agreed. Raise it before they do.
09

Reports and dashboards: why two of them disagree

Important

Serves the outcome the tech brief calls “dashboards leadership trusts” — and the scenario it names: a leadership meeting where two dashboards disagree about the pipeline.

The four building blocks

  • Report type — the most misunderstood object in Salesforce. It defines which objects a report may draw from and which fields are even available. Standard report types cover common shapes; custom report types unlock the rest. Crucially, a report type declares whether children are required: “Accounts with Contacts” silently excludes every Account that has none. Half of all “the numbers are wrong” investigations end here.
  • Report — filters, groupings, and a format: tabular (a list), summary (grouped), matrix (grouped both ways), or joined (multiple blocks). Bucket fields group values on the fly; row-level formulas compute per row.
  • Dashboard — up to a set number of components, each fed by one report.
  • Running user — a dashboard renders through somebody’s record access. By default that is a fixed user, so everyone sees the same numbers regardless of what they can actually see in the CRM. Dynamic dashboards render as the viewer instead.
The checklist for “these two dashboards disagree”
  1. Different running user — one dashboard runs as an executive who sees everything, the other as a BD director who sees their own records.
  2. Different report type — one uses “with Contacts,” the other “with or without.”
  3. Converted leads — Lead reports exclude converted leads by default, so “total leads” and “leads that became projects” come from disjoint populations.
  4. Different date field — Created Date vs. Close Date vs. a custom “Project Landed” date, plus fiscal-year settings that may not match the calendar.
  5. Time zone — reports render in the running user’s time zone; a record created at 11 PM AST lands in a different day for a user set to UTC.
  6. Formula vs. stored field — one component reads a stored value updated by a flow, the other a formula computed live. They diverge the moment the flow fails.
  7. Filter drift — someone added Type = 'New Business' in 2023 and nobody remembers.

The fix that we should be selling is not a better dashboard. It is one written definition per metric — what counts as a qualified lead, what counts as a landed project, which date stamps it — agreed with leadership and then implemented once. That is a governance deliverable with a reporting artifact attached, and it is exactly what a board-reporting nonprofit needs.

Worth knowing exists

CRM Analytics (formerly Einstein/Tableau CRM) and Tableau are separate, licensed products for anything beyond native reporting. Do not assume they have either. Native reports and dashboards will carry a nonprofit of this size perfectly well, and saying so is a cost-credibility signal.

10

Security, sharing, and who can see what

Important

Serves tech-brief problem #6 “user permissions are guesswork” and underpins the proposal’s data-security statement.

Salesforce access is decided in two independent dimensions. Engineers new to the platform routinely conflate them and then cannot explain why a user who “has the permission” still sees nothing.

What this means for an economic development org

  • Routing and visibility are the same design problem. If BD directors own leads by sector and geography, the role hierarchy and sharing model must match that split — or directors will either see everything (no focus) or nothing (no coverage during leave).
  • Queues let a group own records collectively — the right destination for “unrouted leads awaiting triage” rather than dumping them on one person.
  • License types differ. A full Salesforce license, a Platform license, and a community license grant very different access. Never promise a user count without knowing which licenses exist.
  • Deactivate, never delete, users. Deleting is not possible anyway; deactivating preserves history. Reassigning their records is a separate, deliberate step.
  • Setup Audit Trail records configuration changes with user and timestamp, retained for six months. Turn to it first when something changed and nobody knows who.
A concrete offer that costs us nothing and reads as senior

Salesforce ships a Health Check in Setup that scores an org’s security settings against a baseline, and an Optimizer report that flags unused fields, stale automation, and limit pressure. Running both in week one produces a credible, quantified starting picture for the Data Health Review — with no tooling spend and no guessing.

Important

Authentication, briefly

Every integration goes through a Connected App using OAuth 2.0. For a server-to-server sync with no human present, the JWT bearer flow is the correct choice — a signed assertion against a certificate, no stored password, no interactive login, and it survives password rotation. The old username-password flow is deprecated and blocked by default in modern orgs; if we find something depending on it, that is a finding, not a footnote. Connected Apps can also be restricted by IP range and by permitted profiles.

The two limit systems people confuse

Governor limits — per transaction

Enforced on every save, flow run, and Apex execution. They are the reason bulk-safe design is not optional.

  • 100 SOQL queries (synchronous)
  • 50,000 records retrieved by SOQL
  • 150 DML statements; 10,000 records DML’d
  • 6 MB heap sync / 12 MB async
  • 10 s CPU time sync / 60 s async
  • 100 callouts, 120 s total callout time

API allocation — per org, per 24 hours

A rolling daily budget shared by every integration in the org — the HubSpot connector, any middleware, any script we run.

Sized by edition and license count (Enterprise is on the order of 1,000 calls per full license per day on top of a base allocation). Blow through it and everything integration-shaped stops until the window rolls.

Monitor it in Setup → Company Information, or via the limits REST endpoint. Alerting on 80% consumption is a cheap, high-credibility ops deliverable.

Say this in the room

“Before we add anything to the integration surface, I want to see the org’s current API consumption and what is eating it.” It is the correct first question, it is unglamorous, and it is exactly the kind of thing that separates an operator from a salesperson.

Important

Serves tech-brief problem #6 there’s no one to keep the machine running, and directly answers “can you work without touching production?”

Sandbox availability is edition-dependent — orgs get a mix, and Full Copy is not universal. That is why “is a sandbox available?” is one of the questions the tech brief flagged as worth asking. If the answer is no, our approach has to change: staged changes with a backout plan, deployed in a maintenance window, rather than test-then-promote.

The sandbox trap that has burned everyone once

A Full Copy sandbox contains real contact data with real email addresses. If email deliverability is not disabled and a flow fires, you have just emailed live prospects from a test environment. Salesforce defaults new sandboxes to no-access email, but a refreshed one can be reconfigured by anyone. Check it first, every time.

How changes move

  • Change Sets — the point-and-click path. Slow, error-prone, no version control, but it needs no tooling and any admin can run it.
  • Metadata API / Salesforce CLI — sf project deploy start against a git repo. This is the professional path, and it is where our engineering background pays: config becomes reviewable, diffable, and revertible.
  • Unlocked packages — versioned bundles of metadata; the most disciplined option, and usually more ceremony than a small nonprofit needs.
  • 75% Apex test coverage is required to deploy Apex to production. If we inherit undertested code, that is a real, schedulable cost — flag it rather than discover it.
The ops layer the RFP is actually buying

“Integrations fail quietly by default; someone has to be watching.” The monitoring stack we should describe is unglamorous and entirely achievable: the connector’s sync-error queue with an alert on non-zero; API consumption alerting; Setup → Scheduled Jobs and Apex Jobs checked on a cadence; failed-flow error emails routed to a monitored address rather than the last admin who edited the flow; Setup Audit Trail reviewed monthly; and a monthly support log — which the RFP names as a deliverable, making it free points.

Backup — the question they will ask and few vendors answer well

Salesforce’s own guidance is that data protection is a shared responsibility. The Recycle Bin holds deleted records for 15 days. Native weekly data export produces CSVs you must retrieve. Anything better — point-in-time restore, metadata backup, relationship-aware restore — is a paid product (Salesforce Backup, OwnBackup/Own, Gearset, and others). For a nonprofit, the honest answer is usually: scheduled native export to storage they control, plus a documented restore procedure that has actually been tested once. Untested backups are not backups.

Nonprofit specifics — the thing to ask about early

Context

InvestPR is a 501(c)(3). Whether they are on a nonprofit-flavoured Salesforce changes real behaviour, and asking shows we have thought about who they are.

  • Power of Us — Salesforce’s nonprofit program grants eligible 501(c)(3)s ten free licenses plus discounts beyond that. Many nonprofits run on exactly those ten, which is a real constraint on any “just give everyone access” plan and a fact worth knowing when we discuss training scale.
  • NPSP (Nonprofit Success Pack) — a long-established managed package layered on Sales Cloud. It brings its own account model, its own objects (Affiliations, Relationships, General Accounting Units, Recurring Donations), and its own trigger framework. Salesforce has been steering new customers toward the newer Nonprofit Cloud instead, so an org may be on either — or on plain Sales Cloud.
  • Why it matters concretely. NPSP’s Household Account model changes what happens at lead conversion, and its Affiliation object models a person’s relationship to multiple organizations — which, for an investment-attraction agency tracking site selectors who move between firms, is genuinely useful rather than incidental.
  • The likely reality. An economic development organization is B2B: companies, site selectors, projects. Standard Sales Cloud probably fits them better than a donor-shaped package. Their job posting says “the CRM platform, Salesforce” with no further qualifier — so ask, do not assume.
One question, well framed

“Are you running standard Sales Cloud, or a nonprofit package like NPSP or Nonprofit Cloud on top of it?” It takes ten seconds, it is impossible to answer wrong, and it tells you whether an entire layer of managed-package automation is sitting between you and every save.

Grill me: what they will actually ask

Critical

Fifteen questions a CRM-literate stakeholder or their incoming administrator is likely to put to us — with an answer that is short, true, and does not overclaim.

“We’re thinking about consolidating — should we just run everything in HubSpot and drop the CRM?”

Sometimes yes, usually no, and it is not a question to answer before seeing the data. The test is where the relationship of record lives: an EDO tracking multi-year projects with board-reported outcomes needs an object model built for long, multi-stakeholder deals and granular record-level access. Consolidation also throws away years of history and forces a re-migration mid-engagement.

What we would say: “Consolidation is a strategy decision with a real price tag. Our engagement makes the current seam work correctly and gives you the data dictionary you would need before anyone could responsibly evaluate consolidating. If the analysis then says consolidate, you will make that call from evidence rather than frustration.”

Don’t sayOne platform is always better. It reads as a product pitch and invites a fight we cannot win in the room.
“Why do our two dashboards show different pipeline numbers?”

Run the seven-item checklist in module 09 out loud: running user, report type, converted leads, date field, time zone, formula vs. stored field, filter drift. Then land the real point — the fix is not a better dashboard, it is one written definition per metric. This is the single question where a precise answer does the most for our credibility, because everyone in the room has felt this pain and nobody has been given a mechanical explanation of it.

“Can you get rid of our duplicates permanently?”

Two halves, and being explicit about both is the honest answer. Existing duplicates get merged in a controlled pass — rules defined, dry-run in a sandbox, reviewed sample, then executed with a backup taken first. New duplicates get prevented by matching and duplicate rules at the point of creation, plus a scheduled cleanup job for whatever slips through.

The important caveat to volunteer: a rule strict enough to block all duplicates will also block legitimate records and your marketing sync. The design target is “near-zero and self-correcting,” not “zero.”

“How would we know if the integration stopped working?”

Today, most likely you would find out at reporting time, weeks later. That is the default behaviour of every sync — failures land in a queue and nothing escalates. What changes: the sync-error queue gets a threshold alert, API consumption gets a threshold alert, scheduled and Apex jobs get reviewed on a cadence, flow error emails route to a monitored address rather than whoever last edited the flow, and it all lands in the monthly support log the RFP already asks for.

Say“Integrations fail quietly by default. The deliverable isn’t the integration — it’s knowing within hours when it breaks.”
“Can our ad platforms actually see which leads became real projects?”

Yes, and it is the highest-value single fix available. Walk the six steps in module 08. Be precise about the two dependencies people skip: the click ID and UTM values must be captured in hidden form fields at the point of the form fill, and they must survive lead conversion via explicit field mappings. Then CRM stage changes flow back through Enhanced Conversions for Leads, Meta’s Conversions API, and LinkedIn’s Conversions API, and bidding starts optimizing toward qualified projects instead of form fills.

Flag the migration: Google is retiring the legacy click-ID upload path in June 2026, mid-engagement.

“Will you be writing custom code in our system?”

As little as possible, and we will tell you before we do. Recite the ladder: configuration, then declarative automation, then native connectors, then middleware, then code. Every rung we stay above is something your administrator can maintain after we are gone. Where we do write code, it is documented, tested, and handed over.

Why this landsThey are hiring a CRM Administrator concurrently. This answer says we are building their capability, not our indispensability — README decision D5.
“How do you make changes without breaking what we have?”

Sandbox first, wherever a sandbox exists — build and test against a copy, then deploy the metadata. Changes go out in reviewed batches with a documented backout. Data operations get a full export taken immediately before, a dry run on a sample, and a spot-check afterwards. If no sandbox is available, we say so up front and shift to staged changes in an agreed window, which is slower and we will price it that way.

“Who owns which field? Marketing and BD keep overwriting each other.”

This is the field dictionary’s owner system column, and it is the deliverable that ends the argument. One field, one authoritative system, written down and enforced in the connector’s mapping direction. Two-way sync is a deliberate exception for a handful of fields, never a default. Where two teams genuinely need different values, the answer is two fields with different names, not one contested field.

“How long does the data cleanup take?”

The audit is fast and fixed; the remediation scales with what the audit finds. That is why our proposal states the assumptions it is priced against — record counts, user counts, and whether there is a backlog of known sync errors. If those turn out to be materially different, we re-baseline with you rather than absorb it silently or surprise you with a change order.

Why we phrase it this wayDecision D1 — we did not ask InvestPR sizing questions, so every scope variable is an explicit stated assumption. That turns a gap into evidence of cost clarity, which is 20% of the score.
“Do we have to buy more tools?”

Probably one, possibly none. Native duplicate and matching rules, native reports, Health Check, Optimizer, and the platform’s own monitoring cover most of this at no cost. The one category worth paying for is scheduled cross-system data hygiene, because that is the difference between a cleanup that holds and a cleanup that decays. We will make the case with a number, and you can decline it — the architecture does not depend on it.

“How will you work with the CRM Administrator we’re hiring?”

They own the system day to day; we are the architecture and attribution layer above that role. Concretely: we hand over the data dictionary, the automation inventory, the runbooks, and the SOPs, and we work alongside them so the knowledge lands in a person rather than a PDF. Their success is our exit criterion, not a threat to our renewal.

Say“Counterintuitively, a team that doesn’t need us is what earns the next engagement.”
“Is our data safe? Where does it go?”

The data stays in their systems. We work in their org under named accounts with least-privilege access, we do not extract data to our own infrastructure except where an agreed migration or backup requires it, and access is revoked at engagement end. Anything sent to an ad platform for conversion feedback is hashed and limited to what the platform requires — and we would want a short, written agreement about consent and what is permitted to leave, before we build it.

“What about Agentforce / AI in the CRM?”

Genuinely interesting, and entirely downstream of this engagement. Every AI feature on a CRM is a function of the data underneath it — an assistant reading a database with three fields for the same fact and four spellings of Puerto Rico will produce confident, wrong answers faster than a human could. The work in this RFP is the prerequisite. We would rather earn that conversation in six months with a clean data foundation behind it.

Don’tLead with AI. We are an AI-literate firm bidding a data-quality and integration engagement; leading with AI reads as a firm selling what it likes rather than what they asked for.
“You’re two people. What happens if one of you is unavailable?”

Answer with continuity, per decision D2, not with defensiveness. Both principals are on the engagement — there is no ramp-up, no internal handoff, and no staffing-substitution clause where the people who sold the work vanish after kickoff. Everything we build is documented as we build it, in their systems, so the work is legible to their administrator from week one rather than at the end. Specialist contractors are engaged by need, not to pad a bench.

“Have you done this on our exact platform?”

State our own history plainly — Salesforce on the growth team at Zocdoc, HubSpot at Masterworks running the marketing stack for up to $4M a month in paid media, plus Google, Meta, and LinkedIn advertising, GA, and GTM. That is decision D3: platform neutrality applies to their stack, never to ours. Then bring it back to the seam, which is the actual subject: “The specific work here is the integration between a CRM and a marketing platform, and that seam behaves the same way whichever two products sit on either side of it.”
