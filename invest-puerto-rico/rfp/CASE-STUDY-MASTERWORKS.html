The situation

Selling a new asset class, one phone call at a time

Masterworks lets individual investors buy shares in blue-chip paintings — a product nobody wakes up searching for. Growth meant paid media at serious scale, a long consideration cycle, and a regulated funnel that ended not on a website but in a phone call with a human advisor and, weeks later, a funded investment.

That shape — expensive audiences, a decision that happens offline, a team of humans in the middle, and a board that wants spend tied to real outcomes — is the same shape as an economic-development pipeline. The logos differ; the physics don't.

Our principals built and ran this machine together: Hai as co-founder and CMO grew the marketing organization from zero to twenty-five people; Robby, as principal software engineer, built the integration and automation layer underneath it.

The build

One pipe in, one record at the center, one loop back out

The stack was deliberately hub-and-spoke: every event flowed through Segment on the way in, every fact about a person lived in HubSpot at the center, and every valuable outcome flowed back out to the ad platforms. Why this matters here

Same problems, different logos

Every failure mode named in InvestPR's RFP is one we designed against at Masterworks — some because we hit it first and fixed it, some because the architecture made it impossible from day one. The mapping is one-to-one:

The concern in InvestPR's RFPHow the Masterworks build answered it
Data foundation that decays after cleanupA versioned tracking plan governed every event and property — names, allowed values, and one owning system per field. Schema violations were blocked at the pipe, not cleaned up after the fact. Hygiene was enforced by machinery, not memory.
Marketing and the CRM disagree about who a lead isSegment's identity resolution stitched anonymous browsing to the known person at signup, and HubSpot held the one canonical record. Braze, analytics, and the ad platforms were all downstream consumers of the same identity — there was no second version of a person to disagree about.
Ad spend can't see which leads became real outcomesThe dashed loop: funded-investor events flowed back to Meta and Google matched by click ID and hashed email. Bidding optimized toward people who actually invested — not people who fill out forms — and converted spend became suppression lists so we stopped paying to re-reach them.
Meetings and outreach happen off the recordAdvisor calls were booked, routed, and logged on the contact automatically. "When did we last talk to them?" was a lookup, not an investigation — at 25 people and thousands of conversations a month.
Every workflow has three unofficial versionsMessaging logic lived in exactly one place (Braze journeys); money-moment logic lived in exactly one place (the automation service). Every automation was inventoried and diagrammed, so "what fires when" had one answer.
Integrations fail silently; nobody keeps the machine runningThe custom service was built like production software because it was production software: monitored, alerting, with runbooks. A broken sync paged an engineer the same hour — it was never discovered a month later at reporting time.
Knowledge lives in people's headsThe tracking plan, the automation inventory, and the runbooks were the documentation — new marketers and engineers onboarded from artifacts, not shadowing. A 0→25 team can't run any other way.

Outcomes

What the loop paid for

The architecture's return showed up in three places: cheaper acquisition (bidding on real outcomes), faster response (automation at the moments that matter), and a marketing budget that could be defended to a board with CRM-verified numbers. Figures marked ◆ are illustrative pending confirmation:

  • Daily capital raised grew from roughly $2K to $1M◆ over the build-out of this system, with paid media scaling to $4M/month along the way.
  • Cost per funded investor dropped ~35%◆ after bid optimization moved from signup events to funded-investor events sent back through the loop.
  • ~30% of funded conversions◆ were attributable only through server-side events — conversions browser pixels missed entirely (ad blockers, iOS privacy changes, the weeks-long offline gap).
  • Speed-to-lead measured in seconds, not days: high-intent moments (an abandoned funding flow, a booked call) triggered SMS and advisor follow-up automatically.
  • One vocabulary from ad click to board deck: because every system consumed the same events and the same identity, the marketing dashboard, the product funnel, and the finance model never disagreed about what a "funded investor" was.

The transferable pattern

What this means for an organization like InvestPR

Nothing about this architecture is specific to selling art. The pattern — one instrumented pipe in, one canonical record at the center, documented glue with an owner, and valuable outcomes looped back to the platforms that sourced them — transfers to any organization whose real conversions happen offline, weeks after the click, through a team of humans. That is precisely an economic-development pipeline: the "funded investment" becomes a qualified project; the advisory call becomes a BD director's meeting with a site selector; the board deck becomes jobs-and-capital KPIs.

The hub here happened to be HubSpot. The pattern is indifferent to which CRM sits at the center — what matters is that exactly one system is canonical, that everything else reads and writes through documented paths, and that someone owns the glue. That is the system InvestPR's RFP describes wanting, and it is a system our principals have already built, operated, and been accountable for at consumer scale.
